The idea underneath U @ V is simpler than the notation suggests: every entry of the result is just one row of U dotted with one column of V. That's it. The whole matrix is produced by doing this, once, for every (row, column) pairing.

Why this shape, and no other

U is 2×3, V is 3×2. The multiplication is only legal because the "inner" dimensions match — U.shape[1] == V.shape[0] (both 3) — which is exactly the assert from your matrix_matrix_multiplication function. That shared inner dimension (3) is the length both the row of U and the column of V must have, since they're being fed into a plain dot product underneath.

The "outer" dimensions survive into the result: U's row count (2) becomes the result's row count, and V's column count (2) becomes the result's column count. Nothing about the inner dimension (3) shows up in the output shape — it disappears, consumed by the dot product.

Mapping this back to the loop

def matrix_matrix_multiplication(U, V):
    assert U.shape[1] == V.shape[0]

    num_rows = U.shape[0]
    num_cols = V.shape[1]
    result = np.zeros((num_rows, num_cols))

    for i in range(num_cols):
        vi = V[:, i]                                  # column i of V
        result[:, i] = matrix_vector_multiplication(U, vi)   # → column i of result

    return result

Each pass of the loop does one column of the visualization above: it takes column i of V, runs it through matrix_vector_multiplication (which itself dots every row of U against that column), and drops the resulting vector into column i of the output. Two nested dot products — matrix-matrix is matrix-vector repeated once per column of V, and matrix-vector is vector-vector repeated once per row of U.
